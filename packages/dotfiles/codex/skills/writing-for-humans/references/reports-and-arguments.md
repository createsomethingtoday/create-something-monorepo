# Reports and Arguments

Use this reference to organize reports, proposals, arguments, case studies, presentations, and teaching material. Select only the methods needed by the artifact.

## Answer-First Structure

For a report or proposal:

1. State the answer, conclusion, or recommendation first.
2. Group two to four supporting reasons beneath it.
3. Keep supporting reasons at the same logical level.
4. Order them by time, importance, cause, process, or another visible rule.
5. Place evidence, examples, qualifications, and limits beneath the reason they support.

The point above should summarize the points below it. Material that fits nowhere is either irrelevant or evidence that the structure is incomplete.

Do not expose the full discovery journey unless that journey is evidence. Give the reader the result of the reasoning before its history.

## Paragraph Job

Use this default shape when it fits:

```text
Claim
→ Evidence or example
→ Interpretation
→ Connection to the next point
```

Do not leave evidence uninterpreted. State why it changes the reader's understanding or decision.

## Argument

For an argument or research article, make the conversation visible:

1. **They say:** State the existing position, assumption, or problem fairly.
2. **I say:** State the response or contribution.
3. **Because:** Give reasons and evidence.
4. **Some may object:** Address the strongest relevant counterargument.
5. **So what:** Explain the consequence or significance.

Use the sequence as reasoning support, not as mandatory headings.

## Narrative Movement

Use And-But-Therefore when an introduction needs movement:

- **And:** establish the accepted situation
- **But:** introduce the problem, tension, or gap
- **Therefore:** present the response, question, or direction

Do not manufacture conflict. Skip the pattern when a direct answer already gives the reader enough reason to continue.

## Memorability Check

Use SUCCESs only after the structure is clear and the claims are supported:

- **Simple:** one discernible core idea
- **Unexpected:** a useful break from routine expectation
- **Concrete:** something the reader can picture or observe
- **Credible:** sufficient evidence or authority
- **Emotional:** a real reason the audience cares
- **Stories:** action or events when they genuinely clarify the idea

Do not add drama, invented anecdotes, or decorative stories to satisfy the checklist.
